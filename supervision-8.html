Optional: pH, PCO₂ and bicarbonate

≈ 15 min

Use the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation in R to see why the bicarbonate buffer works so well, and draw three acid–base disturbances on a pH–bicarbonate diagram.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with the second pH lecture, and the first.

The Henderson–Hasselbalch equation for the bicarbonate buffer links the three things measured in a sample of arterial blood: pH = 6.1 + log10([HCO3−] / (0.03 × PCO2)), with HCO3− in mM and PCO2 in mmHg (0.03 is the solubility of CO2, in mM per mmHg).

pH, PCO₂ and bicarbonate in R
  1. Write the equation as a function ph <- function(hco3, pco2) 6.1 + log10(hco3 / (0.03 * pco2)) , and try it with normal values ph(24, 40) .
  2. Add 10 mmol of a strong acid to each litre of ECF. It reacts with HCO3−, which falls from 24 to 14 mM, and makes 10 mM of CO2. Compare the pH in a closed system, where the CO2 stays; an open one, where the lungs keep the PCO2 at 40 mmHg; and one with respiratory compensation, where the PCO2 falls by 1.2 mmHg for each mM that HCO3− fell, to 28 mmHg: c(closed = 6.1 + log10(14 / (0.03 * 40 + 10)), open = ph(14, 40), compensated = ph(14, 28))
  3. Draw a pH–bicarbonate diagram: a range of pH pH <- seq(7, 7.7, by = 0.01) , and the line for each of three PCO2s (an isobar) matplot(pH, outer(10^(pH - 6.1), 0.03 * c(20, 40, 80)), type = "l", lty = 1:3, col = 1, xlab = "pH", ylab = "Plasma bicarbonate (mM)") , with a legend legend("topleft", c("PCO2 20 mmHg", "40 mmHg", "80 mmHg"), lty = 1:3) .
  4. Mark the normal state and the two acidoses on it points(c(ph(24, 40), ph(14, 40), ph(14, 28)), c(24, 14, 14), pch = 19) .
  5. At altitude, hyperventilation lowers the PCO2 to 30 mmHg; over a few days, the kidneys excrete HCO3−, which falls by about 4 mM for each 10 mmHg fall in PCO2: c(acute = ph(24, 30), compensated = ph(20, 30))
  6. The lecture’s question: a pH of 7.30, HCO3− 40 mM and PCO2 80 mmHg. Check that they fit together ph(40, 80) .
The pK of the bicarbonate buffer is 6.1, far from 7.4, so in a beaker it would be a poor buffer. What do your three numbers show about why it works so well in the body?
In a closed system the pH falls to about 6.2, which is fatal: the CO2 made from the HCO3− stays, so the ratio of HCO3− to CO2 collapses from 20 to about 1.25. In the body the system is open: the lungs remove the CO2 and hold the PCO2 at 40 mmHg, so the pH falls only to about 7.17 (physiological buffering). The chemoreceptors then drive ventilation up, the PCO2 falls to about 28 mmHg, and the pH rises to about 7.32 (respiratory compensation). The kidneys finish the job by making new HCO3−, to replace what was used up. The buffer is powerful because both of its members are regulated: CO2 by the lungs, HCO3− by the kidneys. (In the body, about half of an acid load is buffered at first by phosphate and proteins, which this calculation leaves out.)
At altitude, what is the pH once the kidneys have compensated? Give it to two decimal places.
About 7.45, from 7.53. The hyperventilation driven by hypoxia (through the peripheral chemoreceptors) lowers the PCO2, a respiratory alkalosis, which itself limits the hyperventilation, because the central chemoreceptors sense the alkaline CSF. Over a few days the kidneys excrete HCO3− (they secrete less H+, and the type B intercalated cells secrete HCO3−), so the pH returns towards normal and ventilation can rise further: ventilatory acclimatisation. On your diagram, the point moves along the isobar for the low PCO2.

Optional: the lungs and the kidneys

Run a diagram of an acid load in which the lungs and the kidneys share the work of regulating pH, then take away each in turn.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with the second pH lecture, and the respiratory lectures on the control of breathing.

The diagram is a simple model of a sustained extra load of non-volatile acid, which starts at 6 hours and goes on. Every level is relative to normal (1): an [H+] of 1 is 40 nM (pH 7.4), 1.1 is 44 nM (pH 7.35) and 1.4 is 56 nM (pH 7.25).

The lungs and the kidneys
  1. Open the diagram . Follow the arrows: the acid uses up plasma HCO3−, so the [H+] rises; the [H+] drives ventilation through the chemoreceptors, and the kidneys’ production of new HCO3−.
  2. Press Run , and watch the graphs: the first few hours, and then the days that follow. Dragging the slider under the graphs shows you the diagram at any moment of the run.
  3. Now let only the lungs respond. Right-click the arrow from [H⁺] of the blood to the kidneys (or press and hold it), choose Block this arrow, and run it again.
  4. Now let only the kidneys respond. Unblock that arrow (right-click it again). Then click the box Ventilation, choose Fixed: it ignores its inputs in the panel on the right, set Fixed at to 1 (its normal level), and run it again.
In the first run, what happened in the first hour, and what happened over the next few days?
Within an hour, the [H+] rose by about 11% (to a pH of about 7.35), ventilation rose by nearly 30% and the PCO2 fell by nearly 30%: the lungs respond in minutes, but they only limit the change in pH, they don’t correct it. Over the following days, the kidneys’ production of new HCO3− rose (to nearly twice normal), so the plasma HCO3− recovered, the [H+] returned almost to normal, and as it did so, ventilation and the PCO2 returned towards normal too. The lungs compensate, quickly and partly; the kidneys correct, slowly and almost completely, and then the respiratory compensation is no longer needed.
Compare the runs with the lungs alone and with the kidneys alone. What does each show, and what does this simple model leave out?
With the lungs alone, the [H+] stays about 11% high for as long as the acid load lasts, with ventilation about 30% above normal: compensation, but no correction, and at a cost (the work of breathing; a low PCO2 constricts the cerebral vessels; and the PCO2 can’t fall much below 10–15 mmHg however hard you breathe). With the kidneys alone, the [H+] rises by nearly 40% (to a pH of about 7.25) before the kidneys bring it back, over a day or two. So the lungs protect against the first, rapid change, and the kidneys restore the HCO3−. The model leaves out a lot: it’s linear (the real Henderson–Hasselbalch relationship isn’t); the buffers other than HCO3−, inside and outside cells; the liver’s switch from urea to glutamine; the limits of renal compensation (real compensation takes three to five days, and in ketoacidosis the acid can come faster than the kidneys can deal with it); and that the lungs excrete 12–15 mol of CO2 a day, while the kidneys are needed every day to make the HCO3− that the normal 70 mmol of non-volatile acid uses up, so in renal failure an acidosis develops without any extra load.

Weighing, not listing

A title that asks for “the relative importance” of two things wants a judgement, and an essay that describes what the lungs do and then what the kidneys do, and stops, hasn’t answered it. Decide on your answer before you write, and build the essay to support it.

  1. Choose your criteria. How fast does each act? How much can it handle? Does it correct a disturbance, or only limit it? What does each deal with: volatile acid (CO2), non-volatile acid, base? What happens when each fails?
  2. Weigh the two on each criterion, with evidence. Disorders are the evidence here: what happens in respiratory failure, in renal failure, at altitude, in ketoacidosis, after vomiting.
  3. Say where the answer depends on the circumstances, and what it depends on: the timescale, and the source of the disturbance.
  4. Conclude with your judgement, which should follow from what you’ve written, not be a surprise.
