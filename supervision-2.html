Optional: the baroreflex as someone stands up

≈ 15 min

Measure, in the Recorder, how the blood pressure falls when someone stands up, and how the baroreflex brings it back.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with Dr Fraser’s fourth lecture, on the control of arterial blood pressure, and with postural hypotension in his clinical lecture.

When you stand up, about half a litre of blood pools in the veins of your legs. In this recording a healthy young man stood up by himself from lying flat on a tilt table (the table didn’t move), with his blood pressure measured beat by beat at a finger, and an ECG.

The baroreflex, beat by beat
  1. Open the recording, from 16 s before he stands up to 34 s after . A flag marks the moment he stands up, and his heart rate is at the bottom. The pressure rises for a beat or two just before the flag, as he braces himself to get up.
  2. Choose the Calipers . Drag across one beat of the blood pressure from before he stands up, from the lowest point of one pulse to the lowest point of the next, choosing a beat before 37 s. The bar above the traces shows the minimum pressure (the diastolic), the maximum (the systolic) and the mean.
  3. Do the same for a beat at the lowest pressure, a few seconds after he stands up.
By how much did his mean pressure fall, and how long did it take to come back? In terms of ABP = CO × TPR and VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR, why did it fall?
His mean pressure fell from about 65 mmHg (a beat of about 97/53) to about 51 mmHg (about 84/38) within a few seconds, and it was back within about ten seconds of his standing up. He stood up by himself, so in the first seconds the leg muscles that lifted him dilated their arterioles, as at the start of exercise, and TPR fell. Then, as he stayed upright, about half a litre of blood pooled in the veins of his legs, where the hydrostatic pressure is now high. That blood no longer contributes to filling the heart: venous return and RAP fall, by Starling’s law each beat ejects less, and CO falls.
His heart rate rose from about 77 to about 105 a minute within about ten seconds. Which receptors detected the change, by what pathway did the heart rate rise, and what else did the reflex do? Why did the pressure then overshoot for a few beats?
The arterial baroreceptors of the carotid sinus and aortic arch fired less as the pressure fell, and so did the low-pressure receptors of the great veins and atria as central blood volume fell. Less inhibition of the medullary vasomotor areas meant less vagal activity to the sinoatrial node (the fast part of the rise in rate, within a beat or two) and more sympathetic activity: to the heart (rate and contractility), to the arterioles (raising TPR) and to the veins (raising MSFP, and so venous return). The pressure overshot to about 130/70 mmHg because the reflex has delays: the sympathetic constriction of the vessels builds up over several seconds and outlasts the fall it was correcting, so the baroreflex then worked the other way, and his heart rate fell back for a few beats, before both settled, with his heart rate higher than when he lay down.
Postural hypotension is a fall in pressure on standing that the reflex doesn’t correct fast enough. Dr Fraser points out that its key symptom comes from the brain, whereas in a long-lasting hypotension, such as after a haemorrhage, the brain is usually spared. Why the difference?

Optional: haemorrhage and heart failure in a model

≈ 15 min

Bleed, and then weaken the heart of the model of the circulation, and find out why the right atrial pressure tells the two kinds of shock apart.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with Dr Fraser’s fifth lecture, on haemorrhage, and his clinical lecture, on heart failure and shock.

This is the model of the circulation from Supervision 1: MSFP drives venous return through RvR, so that VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR, the heart (a flow source) pumps whatever you set, and ABP = CO × TPR. Pressures are in mmHg, flows in mL/s (83.3 mL/s is 5 L/min). A real heart pumps what returns to it (Starling’s law), so in the body RAP stays close to zero; here you set the heart’s output yourself, which lets you see what the body has to do.

A haemorrhage
  1. Open the model and run it . At rest, 83.3 mL/s returns and is pumped, RAP is about 0 mmHg and ABP 93 mmHg.
  2. Lose about a tenth of the blood. Only about the last fifth of the blood volume stretches the vessels’ walls, so without venoconstriction that roughly halves MSFP. Click Mean systemic filling pressure and set it to 3.5 mmHg.
  3. With the heart still pumping 83.3 mL/s, RAP is now negative, which real veins wouldn’t allow. Click the Heart and set the output that brings RAP back to zero: 41.7 mL/s.
A failing heart
  1. Now put the volume back (MSFP 7 mmHg) and weaken the heart instead. Set the Heart to 50 mL/s (3 L/min), as a failing heart that can’t pump more.
  2. The body sees a low pressure and responds as it would to any fall in pressure: venoconstriction, and the kidneys retain salt and water, which raises the blood volume. Raise MSFP to 14 mmHg, and keep the heart at 50 mL/s. Watch RAP.
Compare the haemorrhage (MSFP 3.5 mmHg, heart at 41.7 mL/s) with the failing heart (heart at 50 mL/s, MSFP 14 mmHg). What are ABP and RAP in each? How would measuring RAP help a doctor tell hypovolaemic from cardiogenic shock?
After the haemorrhage, the output that keeps RAP at zero is only 41.7 mL/s (2.5 L/min), so ABP = CO × TPR falls to about 46.5 mmHg, with RAP about 0. With the failing heart, ABP is also low, about 55.8 mmHg (50 mL/s × 1.116), but RAP is high: about 2.8 mmHg with MSFP at 7, and about 9.8 mmHg once MSFP rises to 14. So both are shock (a low pressure and cardiac output), but in hypovolaemic shock RAP (or central venous pressure) is low, because there isn’t enough blood returning, and in cardiogenic shock it’s high, because the heart can’t pump what returns. That decides the treatment: fluid for the first, and not for the second, where more volume would only raise RAP further.
In the failing heart, raising MSFP from 7 to 14 mmHg didn’t raise the cardiac output at all; it only raised RAP. Why is that a problem, and what does it suggest about how heart failure should be treated?
VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR, and if the heart can’t pump more, the output stays at 50 mL/s, so a higher MSFP can only be balanced by a higher RAP: RAP = MSFP − CO × RvR = 14 − 4.2 = 9.8 mmHg. In a real failing heart the Starling curve is flattened, so a large rise in RAP gives only a small rise in output. Raised venous and atrial pressures raise capillary pressure upstream (it follows venous pressure), so fluid filters into the tissues and lungs: oedema, a hallmark of heart failure. Arteriolar constriction also raises TPR, which makes a failing heart work harder and can lower its output further. So the body’s normal responses to a low pressure make heart failure worse, and most drugs for it block them: diuretics and ACE inhibitors (or angiotensin receptor blockers) lower volume and MSFP, β-blockers reduce sympathetic drive, and vasodilators lower TPR.

This week you hand in four short answers instead of an essay. A short-answer question is marked against a list of points, so it rewards precision and coverage rather than argument. Each of these is marked out of five.

Answering a short-answer question

  1. Read the verb. “Explain” wants mechanisms (why); “outline” wants the main points, in order; “distinguish” wants the differences, side by side.
  2. Count the marks. Five marks means at least five separate, precise points. Bullet points are fine, and quicker to mark.
  3. Make each point earn its mark. A mechanism with its direction (“less vagal activity to the sinoatrial node raises the heart rate”), a number (“0.5–1 L of tissue fluid”), or an equation used (“RAP = MSFP − CO × RvR”) is worth more than a general statement (“the body compensates”).
  4. Answer the question that’s asked. Don’t write everything you know about the topic. A diagram can save words, if it’s labelled.
Two answers to the same question

“Outline the responses to a haemorrhage.”

Answer A: “The baroreceptors detect the fall in blood pressure. The sympathetic nervous system is activated, and the heart rate and blood pressure increase. The kidneys retain water.”

Answer B: “• Lost volume lowers MSFP, so venous return, CO and ABP fall. • Seconds: arterial and low-pressure baroreceptors fire less; less vagal and more sympathetic activity raise heart rate, contractility, TPR and (by venoconstriction) MSFP. • Minutes: lower capillary pressure draws 0.5–1 L of tissue fluid into the blood (autotransfusion), so the haematocrit falls. • Tens of minutes: renin, angiotensin II, aldosterone and ADH, with thirst, restore volume. • Days: plasma proteins (24–48 h) and red cells (erythropoietin, 5–7 days).”

A has three points, none of them wrong, but none precise. B has more than five, each a mechanism with its direction and its timescale, and a number where one helps. That’s what earns the marks.
