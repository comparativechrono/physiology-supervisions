Optional: where does a litre go?

≈ 15 min

Work out, with the calculator, what a litre of fresh water, isotonic saline or seawater does to the osmolality and the volumes of the body’s fluids.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with the fourth lecture, on extracellular fluid volume and the integrated responses.

Use the lecture’s round numbers: 40 L of body water at 300 mOsm/kg, 16 L of it outside the cells (3 L of that is plasma) and 24 L inside them. Water crosses cell membranes easily, so the fluid inside and outside the cells always ends up at the same osmolality. Na+ and Cl− stay outside the cells.

Where does a litre go?
  1. Open the calculator .
  2. A litre of fresh water adds water but no solute, and spreads through all the body water. Work out the new osmolality: the body’s 40 × 300 mOsm, now in 41 L.
  3. The solute outside the cells (16 × 300 mOsm) stays there. Work out the new volume of the extracellular fluid (ECF): that solute divided by the new osmolality.
  4. A litre of isotonic saline adds 300 mOsm and a litre of water, all of it to the ECF, so the osmolality doesn’t change, and no water moves into or out of the cells. Work out the rise in the volume of the ECF, as a percentage.
  5. A litre of seawater adds a litre of water and about 1000 mOsm of salt, which stays in the ECF once it has been absorbed. Work out the new osmolality.
  6. And the new volume of the ECF, which now holds 16 × 300 + 1000 mOsm.
After the litre of fresh water, how much of it is in the ECF, and how much in the plasma? Which of the body’s regulatory systems will notice, and what does it do?
The ECF rises from 16 to 16.4 L, so it holds 0.4 L of the litre, and the cells 0.6 L. The plasma is 3 of the 16 L of ECF, so it gains only about 75 mL: the blood volume barely changes, and the volume receptors see little (the lecture: “no volume regulatory effects”). But the osmolality falls by about 7 mOsm/kg (2.4%), and plasma osmolality is normally held within 282 to 290, so the hypothalamic osmoreceptors notice: the magnocellular neurones swell, their stretch-inactivated cation channels close, they fire less, and less ADH is released from the posterior pituitary. With little ADH, the collecting ducts lose their aquaporin-2 and become impermeable to water, so the dilute fluid leaving the loop of Henle passes on as dilute urine: a water diuresis. Because ADH is released by nerves and cleared quickly, the response is fast, and most of the litre has gone within two or three hours. Reflexes from the gut and liver begin to inhibit ADH even before the water is absorbed (feed-forward).
Isotonic saline expands the ECF by 6.25%, but doesn’t change its osmolality. Why is it excreted so much more slowly than fresh water?
Because the fast system, osmoregulation through the osmoreceptors and ADH, isn’t engaged: nothing has changed for it to correct. Only volume regulation responds, and its output, Na+ excretion, is slow. The plasma gains about 0.2 L (3/16 of the litre), which is sensed by the cardiopulmonary (atrial and great vein) receptors and the arterial baroreceptors, and by the kidney itself. The responses: less renal sympathetic activity; less renin, so less angiotensin II and less aldosterone; more atrial natriuretic peptide; a slightly higher arterial pressure (pressure natriuresis); and diluted plasma proteins, which lower the colloid osmotic pressure in the peritubular capillaries and reduce proximal reabsorption. Together they raise Na+ excretion, and water follows, but aldosterone acts through gene transcription, and the excess takes many hours, up to a day or two, to excrete. Volume regulation is subordinate to osmoregulation: the ECF volume is allowed to vary, while the osmolality isn’t.
After the seawater, what has happened to the cells? Why does drinking seawater leave you more dehydrated than before?
The osmolality rises from 300 to about 317 mOsm/kg (5.7%), so water leaves the cells: they lose 1.3 L (24 to 22.7 L), while the ECF gains 2.3 L (to 18.3 L). So the ECF is expanded but the cells are dehydrated, and osmotic thirst and maximal ADH secretion follow; osmotic thirst overrides the signals from an expanded volume. (At first, before it’s absorbed, the hypertonic seawater draws water into the gut, so the ECF shrinks and there is hypovolaemic thirst too.) To get rid of the salt, the kidney must excrete it. The most concentrated urine a human kidney can make is about 1200 mOsm/kg, but in a maximally concentrating kidney up to half of the medulla’s osmotic pressure is urea, so the NaCl in the urine can’t be as concentrated as it is in seawater. Excreting the salt of a litre of seawater takes more than a litre of urine, so the body loses water overall, and drinking more seawater makes things worse.

Optional: potassium after a meal

≈ 15 min

Work out what the potassium in a meal would do if it stayed in the extracellular fluid, and find the cells that make aldosterone.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with the fifth lecture, on potassium, and the fourth, on aldosterone.

Plasma K+ is held at about 4.5 mM, and above 5.5 mM (hyperkalaemia) it’s dangerous. There’s only about 50–70 mmol of K+ in the ECF, but about 4 mol inside the cells, at 150 mM. A meal rich in fruit and vegetables can easily hold 40 mmol of K+, getting on for half a day’s intake.

Potassium after a meal
  1. Open the calculator .
  2. Suppose the 40 mmol were all absorbed, and stayed in the 16 L of ECF. Work out what the plasma K+ would become, starting from 4.5 mM.
  3. The resting membrane potential of muscle and nerve is close to the K+ equilibrium potential. Work out EK at 37 °C with a plasma K+ of 7 mM and 150 mM inside: 61.5 × log(7 ÷ 150), in mV. (It’s about −94 mV normally, at 4.5 mM.)
  4. Open the adrenal gland at 10× : the capsule, and the cortex just inside it.
  5. Find the zona glomerulosa, which makes aldosterone . It’s a thin, patchy layer of small cells in rounded clusters, just under the capsule, before the long, straight cords of the zona fasciculata.
What would a plasma K+ of 7 mM do, and what stops the K+ in a meal from getting anywhere near it?
EK would rise by about 12 mV, from about −94 to −82 mV, so excitable cells would depolarise. A small depolarisation brings cells nearer threshold, but a sustained one inactivates voltage-gated Na+ channels, so excitability and conduction fall: in the heart, conduction slows, and arrhythmias and cardiac arrest can follow. What prevents it is internal balance: K+ is taken up into cells, mainly muscle and liver, by the Na+/K+-ATPase, which insulin (released by the meal), adrenaline (through β2 receptors) and aldosterone stimulate. 40 mmol is only 1% of the 4 mol already inside the cells. Then, over the next hours, the kidney excretes it: K+ secretion by the principal cells of the distal tubule and collecting duct rises, stimulated directly by the high plasma K+ and by aldosterone. Renal excretion is too slow to deal with a meal’s K+ on its own, which is why people with diabetes (with little insulin) see larger rises.
Aldosterone promotes both Na+ reabsorption and K+ secretion by the same cells. So why doesn’t a fall in blood volume, which raises aldosterone, make you lose K+? And why doesn’t a water diuresis?
In hypovolaemia, more Na+ and water are reabsorbed earlier in the nephron (angiotensin II and the sympathetic nerves act on the proximal tubule; GFR may fall), so less fluid flows through the distal tubule and collecting duct. With a low flow, the K+ secreted builds up in the lumen next to the membrane, and the gradient for further secretion falls. The low flow offsets the high aldosterone, and K+ balance is kept. In a water diuresis the flow is high, which would favour K+ secretion, but ADH is low, and ADH itself increases the K+ permeability of the luminal membrane, so the two effects cancel. The two stimuli to aldosterone secretion differ too: angiotensin II (low volume) and a raised plasma K+, acting directly on the zona glomerulosa.
Adrenalectomised animals given a constant infusion of aldosterone can regulate their ECF volume on a low-Na+ diet, but not their plasma K+. What does this show?

Write your essay, and hand it in

This is the work to hand in this week. You write it in Supervision work, as you will in the online exam, and then hand it in from the same page.

“How does an adult human respond to the ingestion of one litre of (a) fresh water; (b) isotonic saline; (c) seawater?”

An essay in three parts

A title with parts (a), (b) and (c) needs each part answered, but three separate mini-essays usually make a weak essay: they repeat themselves, and they miss the point of putting the three together. The point here is a comparison: the same body, the same volume drunk, three different disturbances, and so three different combinations of the osmoregulatory and volume-regulating systems.

  1. An introduction that sets up the tools. The compartments and their volumes; the two systems, osmoregulation (osmoreceptors, ADH and thirst: fast, and in charge) and volume regulation (volume receptors, the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, ANP, the renal nerves and the physical factors: slower, and subordinate).
  2. The same skeleton for each drink. What it does to osmolality and to the volumes (with numbers); what detects it; what the effectors do; how fast; and where the body ends up.
  3. Comparisons as you go. Saline engages the system that fresh water doesn’t; seawater engages both, in opposite directions, and shows which one wins.
  4. A conclusion that answers the question. What the three cases show together about how the two systems are organised.
Which plan for this essay is best?
After a litre of isotonic saline, which of these would you not expect?
