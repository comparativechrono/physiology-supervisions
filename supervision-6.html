The renal cortex
  1. Open the renal cortex at 4× .
  2. Find a renal corpuscle and click on it : a knot of capillaries, the glomerulus, in a cup, Bowman’s capsule.
  3. Find Bowman’s space, between the glomerulus and the capsule, where the filtrate collects .
  4. Find a proximal convoluted tubule . Its cells are tall and stain pink, and its lumen looks fuzzy, because of the brush border.
  5. Measure the diameter of a renal corpuscle with the measuring tool .
Your renal corpuscle is probably about 200 µm across. A human kidney has about a million of them, so the two kidneys about two million. If the GFR is 125 mL/min, how much does each glomerulus filter, in nanolitres a minute?
125 mL/min ÷ 2,000,000 = 62.5 × 10−6 mL/min, about 60 nL a minute for each glomerulus (the number of nephrons varies a lot between people, so this is only a guide). That’s why micropuncture, collecting nanolitres of fluid from single nephrons, was the key to finding out what the tubules do.
The cells of the proximal tubule stain pink with eosin largely because they’re packed with mitochondria, and they have a brush border of microvilli. How do these features fit what the proximal tubule does, and what has this to do with the “expenditure by the tubule cells” in your essay’s title?
The proximal tubule reabsorbs about two-thirds of the filtered water and Na+, and nearly all the glucose, amino acids and much of the bicarbonate. The brush border gives a huge apical surface area for the transporters (Na+-glucose cotransporters, Na+/H+ exchangers). The mitochondria provide ATP for the basolateral Na+/K+-ATPase, which keeps the cell’s Na+ low, so Na+ enters across the apical membrane down its gradient, bringing other solutes with it, and water follows osmotically. So reabsorption is driven by active transport, and it costs energy: the kidneys use 7–10% of the body’s O2 at rest, and their O2 consumption rises in proportion to the Na+ they reabsorb. In stopped-flow perfusions, metabolic poisons (2,4-dinitrophenol) and ouabain cut the reabsorption of fluid from the proximal tubule by about two-thirds (Schatzmann and colleagues, 1958).

Optional: clearance and the transport maximum

≈ 15 min

Work out the clearances of inulin, glucose and PAH over a range of plasma concentrations, and answer an old exam question.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with the second lecture, on clearance and the transport maximum.

Dr Sage’s slides include “an exam question from the good old days (of IA Physiology)”: why is it that at high plasma concentrations the clearances of glucose, inulin and para-aminohippuric acid are similar? You can see the answer by working out the three clearances in R, over a wide range of plasma concentrations. All three are freely filtered. Inulin is neither reabsorbed nor secreted, glucose is reabsorbed by a carrier with a transport maximum (Tmax), and PAH is secreted by one.

Three clearances, in R
  1. Set the values: a GFR of 125 mL/min, an effective renal plasma flow of 650 mL/min, and the Tmax of glucose (375 mg/min, from the handout) and of PAH (about 80 mg/min): gfr <- 125; erpf <- 650; tm_glucose <- 375; tm_pah <- 80
  2. A range of plasma concentrations, in mg/mL: P <- seq(0.05, 10, by = 0.05)
  3. Work out the clearances of glucose and PAH. Clearance is the rate of excretion divided by the plasma concentration. Glucose excreted is what is filtered (GFR × P) beyond the Tmax; PAH excreted is what is filtered plus what is secreted, up to all of the plasma that flows past the tubules: glucose <- pmax(0, gfr * P - tm_glucose) / P; pah <- pmin(erpf * P, gfr * P + tm_pah) / P
  4. Plot all three matplot(P, cbind(gfr, glucose, pah), type = "l", lty = 1:3, col = 1, xlab = "Plasma concentration (mg/mL)", ylab = "Clearance (mL/min)") , and add a legend legend("right", c("inulin", "glucose", "PAH"), lty = 1:3) .
  5. What are the three clearances at the highest concentration, 10 mg/mL? round(c(inulin = gfr, glucose = tail(glucose, 1), pah = tail(pah, 1)), 1)
Why do the three clearances converge at high plasma concentrations? Answer the old exam question.
At 10 mg/mL they’re 125 (inulin), 87.5 (glucose) and 133 mL/min (PAH), and they’d come closer still at higher concentrations. Inulin’s clearance is the GFR whatever its concentration, because it’s only filtered. Glucose is filtered and reabsorbed by carriers that saturate at their Tmax; at high concentrations the filtered load is far above the Tmax, so the reabsorbed part becomes a small fraction of what’s filtered, and the clearance rises towards the GFR. PAH is filtered and secreted by carriers that also saturate; at low concentrations nearly all of it is cleared from the plasma flowing past the tubules (its clearance is the effective renal plasma flow, 650 mL/min), but at high concentrations the secreted part becomes small beside what is filtered, so its clearance falls towards the GFR. Carrier-mediated transport is limited, filtration isn’t, so at high concentrations filtration dominates for all three.
In this model, at what plasma glucose concentration, in mg/mL, does glucose first appear in the urine? The handout says glucose appears at about 2 mg/mL in a real person. Why the difference?
In the model, glucose is excreted once the filtered load exceeds the Tmax: GFR × P > 375, so P > 375 / 125 = 3 mg/mL (about 17 mM). Real kidneys show splay: the curves of reabsorption and excretion bend gradually instead of meeting the Tmax sharply, because nephrons differ (in length, in the number of carriers, and in their GFR), so some saturate at lower plasma concentrations than others. Glucose therefore starts to appear in the urine at a lower concentration, the renal threshold, of about 2 mg/mL (10–11 mM). That’s why glucosuria is a sign of poorly controlled diabetes.

“What is the evidence that the kidneys produce urine by a process of ultrafiltration, followed by fluid reabsorption, involving expenditure by the tubule cells?”

Using experimental evidence

This title asks for evidence, so an essay that describes how the kidney works, however accurately, misses the point. Every paragraph should rest on an experiment or an observation, and say what it shows. A useful pattern, for each piece of evidence:

  1. The claim it supports: “The fluid in Bowman’s space is an ultrafiltrate of plasma.”
  2. The experiment, briefly: who, on what, and what they did. “Wearn and Richards (1924) collected fluid from Bowman’s capsule of frogs by micropuncture.”
  3. The result, with a number if there is one. “It had the composition of plasma, but almost no protein.”
  4. The inference: why the result supports the claim, and what else it rules out.
  5. Its limits, where they matter: frogs aren’t mammals; one nephron isn’t the whole kidney.

The title has three claims, so the essay can take them in turn: ultrafiltration (micropuncture of the capsule; the dependence of filtration on pressure; the structure of the barrier), reabsorption (GFR, measured with inulin, is 125 mL/min but urine flow about 1; changes in the composition of the fluid along the tubule; inulin concentration rising along the proximal tubule while the osmolality stays the same; the split-oil-drop experiments), and expenditure by the tubule cells (reabsorption against gradients, metabolic poisons and ouabain cutting fluid reabsorption, the O2 the kidney consumes).

Which sentence uses evidence best?
Schatzmann and colleagues (1958) perfused single proximal tubules with fluid held between oil drops. In 20 minutes, 27% of the fluid was reabsorbed normally, but only about 10% with 2,4-dinitrophenol or with ouabain. What does this show?
