The adrenal gland

Where cortisol is made
  1. Open the adrenal gland at 2× . You’re looking at a slice across the gland: a capsule, the cortex inside it, and the medulla in the middle.
  2. Find the zona fasciculata, which makes cortisol, and click on it . Its cells are in long columns, and look pale and foamy because they’re full of lipid droplets: cholesterol ester, the raw material of steroids.
  3. Now find the medulla .
Blood flows inwards, from the capsule through the cortex to the veins of the medulla. Why might it matter that the chromaffin cells of the medulla receive blood that has just passed through the zona fasciculata?
The blood reaching the medulla carries a high concentration of cortisol, much higher than in the general circulation. Cortisol induces PNMT, the enzyme that methylates noradrenaline to adrenaline, so the portal arrangement helps the medulla make adrenaline rather than noradrenaline. It ties the two parts of the gland, of different embryological origins (mesoderm and neural crest), into one stress organ: the medulla answers within seconds to sympathetic preganglionic nerves, and the cortex within minutes to ACTH.

The axis as a diagram

The diagram has the three hormones of the axis, each at its resting level (1). Stress drives CRH from the hypothalamus, which drives ACTH, which drives cortisol, and cortisol feeds back on both the hypothalamus and the pituitary.

Run it, then break it
  1. Open the diagram and run it . Stress starts at 2 hours.
  2. Destroy the adrenal cortex, as in Addison’s disease. Click the box Cortisol, choose Knocked out for it in the panel on the right, and run it again. Then put it back to normal, choose Fixed for it instead, at a level of 3, as in a patient taking a glucocorticoid drug, and run it again.
With the adrenal cortex knocked out, what happened to ACTH, and with cortisol fixed at 3? What would you see in a patient with each condition, and why?
With no cortisol, there’s no negative feedback, so CRH and ACTH rise: ACTH settles at about 2.7 times its resting level in the diagram. In Addison’s disease the high ACTH (and other products of pro-opiomelanocortin, POMC, the precursor it’s cut from, including MSH) darkens the skin. The patient also lacks aldosterone, because the whole cortex is destroyed: salt loss, a low blood volume and pressure, and a high plasma K+. Without cortisol they can’t maintain blood glucose in a fast or exercise, nor vascular responsiveness to catecholamines (a permissive effect), so a stress such as an infection can cause a fatal collapse. With cortisol fixed at 3, the feedback suppresses ACTH to about a sixth of its resting level (0.17). In a patient on glucocorticoids, the low ACTH lets the zona fasciculata atrophy (ACTH is trophic to it), so stopping the drug suddenly leaves them without cortisol until the axis recovers, which can take months. That’s why the dose is tapered.
Which observation from the lecture is the best evidence that cortisol controls ACTH by negative feedback?

Optional: growth hormone and the steroids, in 3D

≈ 15 min

See how one growth hormone molecule switches on a pair of receptors, and why the kidney can tell aldosterone from cortisol.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with the second and third lectures.

Growth hormone and its receptors
  1. Open growth hormone with its receptors , and colour it by chain . One chain is growth hormone, a bundle of four helices. The other two are the parts of two receptor molecules that stick out of the cell.
One molecule of growth hormone binds two receptors, using two different sites on its surface. The lecture says that the receptors exist as pre-formed dimers. What does the hormone do to them, and how is the signal passed into the cell? Can you think how a drug might block the receptor using this structure?
Growth hormone binds first through a high-affinity site (site 1) to one receptor, then through a second site (site 2) to the other. Because the two receptors are already paired, the hormone doesn’t bring them together so much as rotate and reposition them relative to each other. That moves their intracellular parts so that the JAK2 kinases bound to them phosphorylate each other and the receptor’s tyrosines, which recruits STAT5 and other signalling proteins (and the growth effects partly through IGF-1). A drug can exploit the two sites: pegvisomant is growth hormone with site 1 strengthened and site 2 disabled, so it occupies the receptor pair without activating it. It is used to treat acromegaly.
The steroids
  1. Open the glucocorticoid receptor’s hormone-binding part, holding dexamethasone , and show the drug as spheres . It sits deep inside the protein, which closes round it.
  2. Now compare cortisol with aldosterone . Look at carbons 11 and 18: cortisol has an OH on carbon 11, and aldosterone has an aldehyde (CHO) on carbon 18 as well.
The mineralocorticoid receptor binds cortisol as well as aldosterone, and there’s far more cortisol in the plasma. How do the cells of the collecting duct respond to aldosterone and not to cortisol? Why can eating a lot of liquorice raise the blood pressure?
The cells of the distal tubule and collecting duct express 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11β-HSD2), which oxidises the 11-OH of cortisol to a keto group, making cortisone, which doesn’t bind the receptor. Aldosterone escapes, because its 18-aldehyde folds onto its 11-OH (forming a hemiacetal), which protects it from the enzyme. So the receptor in these cells sees aldosterone. Liquorice contains glycyrrhetinic acid, which inhibits 11β-HSD2: cortisol then occupies the mineralocorticoid receptor, the cells reabsorb too much Na+ (and lose K+), extracellular volume rises, and so does the blood pressure, with renin and aldosterone suppressed (apparent mineralocorticoid excess).
Cortisol binds a receptor inside the cell, and growth hormone a receptor in the membrane. What follows from that for how quickly each acts?

“Compare and contrast the metabolic roles of growth hormone and cortisol.”

Writing a comparison

“Compare and contrast” asks for similarities and differences, and the best essays also explain them: why two hormones that both raise blood glucose in a fast should differ in when, and how, they act. Two ways to organise it:

  • Hormone by hormone: all about growth hormone, then all about cortisol, then a comparison at the end. It’s easy to write, but the comparison tends to be squeezed into the last paragraph, or left to the reader.
  • Point by point: each paragraph takes one aspect (what triggers secretion, the effects on glucose, on fat, on protein, the timescale, what happens in excess or deficiency) and compares the two hormones on it. It’s harder to plan, but every paragraph compares, so it usually scores better.

Whichever you choose, plan it as a table first: one row for each aspect, a column for each hormone, and a column for why they’re alike or differ. Then write from the table.

  • What triggers them? Both rise in hypoglycaemia and in stress or exercise. What else releases each one: amino acids, low free fatty acids, sleep, the time of day?
  • Glucose, fat and protein. Both antagonise insulin and spare glucose for the brain. Where do they differ, above all over protein?
  • The paradox. Growth hormone is released in fasting yet makes things grow. How does the body stop it promoting growth when there’s no food? (Insulin, IGF-1 and its binding proteins, FGF21, thyroid hormone.)
  • Timescale and mechanism. A membrane receptor and JAK2, against an intracellular receptor and transcription. Pulses, half-lives and binding proteins.
  • What goes wrong. Acromegaly and Cushing’s syndrome both cause diabetes. Deficiency of both, in a fast or strenuous exercise, can cause fatal hypoglycaemia.
