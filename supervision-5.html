Optional: haemoglobin, anaemia and carbon monoxide

≈ 15 min

Look at haemoglobin in 3D, then use its dissociation curve, in R, to find out why carbon monoxide is far more dangerous than an anaemia.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with the fourth lecture, on the transport of respiratory gases in the blood.

Haemoglobin
  1. Open haemoglobin , colour it by chain , and show its four haem groups as spheres . This is deoxyhaemoglobin: two α and two β chains, each holding a haem.
In deoxyhaemoglobin there’s a cavity between the two β chains, along the central axis of the molecule, which closes up when it binds O2. 2,3-BPG binds in it. What does that do to the dissociation curve, and why does it matter at altitude and in the fetus?
2,3-BPG binds to the positively charged groups lining the cavity between the β chains, but only in the deoxy (T) form, which it stabilises. That lowers haemoglobin’s affinity for O2 and shifts the dissociation curve to the right (a higher P50), so more O2 is given up at the PO2 of the tissues. At altitude, red cells make more 2,3-BPG within a day or two, which helps unloading. Fetal haemoglobin has γ chains in place of β, which bind 2,3-BPG poorly, so its curve lies to the left of the mother’s, and O2 moves from her blood to the fetus’s across the placenta (in pregnancy, maternal 2,3-BPG rises, shifting her curve further right).
Anaemia and carbon monoxide, in R
  1. Write the dissociation curve as a function, the Hill equation, with P50 as an argument: sat <- function(po2, p50 = 26.8) po2^2.7 / (po2^2.7 + p50^2.7)
  2. And the O2 content, in mL per litre, with the handout’s values: content <- function(po2, hb = 150, p50 = 26.8) 1.39 * hb * sat(po2, p50) + 0.03 * po2
  3. Plot the normal curve curve(content(x), 0, 120, ylim = c(0, 210), xlab = "PO2 (mmHg)", ylab = "O2 content (mL per litre)") , then add an anaemia with half the haemoglobin (dashed), and carbon monoxide occupying half of it, which also shifts the curve of what’s left to the left (P50 about 15 mmHg; dotted): curve(content(x, hb = 75), add = TRUE, lty = 2); curve(content(x, hb = 75, p50 = 15), add = TRUE, lty = 3)
  4. How much O2 does each litre of blood give up as its PO2 falls from 100 mmHg (arterial) to 40 mmHg (mixed venous)? release <- function(...) content(100, ...) - content(40, ...); c(normal = release(), anaemia = release(hb = 75), CO = release(hb = 75, p50 = 15))
How much O2 does a litre of blood give up between 100 and 40 mmHg in each case, to the nearest mL? Both the anaemia and the carbon monoxide halve the O2 that the arterial blood carries, so why is carbon monoxide so much worse?
About 49 mL normally, about 25 mL in the anaemia, and only about 8 mL with carbon monoxide. In both, the arterial content is roughly halved. But in the anaemia the curve keeps its shape, so the blood still gives up its O2 at the PO2 of the tissues. Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin about 200 times more tightly than O2, and once it occupies some haems, the remaining haems hold their O2 more tightly too (the curve shifts left and loses its sigmoid shape), so very little is released until the tissue PO2 falls very low. Worse, the patient doesn’t hyperventilate, because the arterial PO2 and PCO2 are normal, the skin looks pink, and a pulse oximeter reads carboxyhaemoglobin as oxyhaemoglobin and gives a normal saturation.

Patient A: FEV1/FVC 0.39, a TLC above predicted, and a low diffusing capacity. What pattern is this, and what disease is most likely?
Patient B: FEV1/FVC 0.88, a low TLC and a low diffusing capacity. What pattern is this?
Patients A and B both have a low diffusing capacity, for different reasons. Using Fick’s law, explain the reason in each.
Fick’s law: the rate of diffusion across the barrier is proportional to its area and to the difference in partial pressure, and inversely proportional to its thickness (times a constant for the gas). In emphysema (A), the walls between alveoli are destroyed, so the area for exchange (and the capillaries in it) falls. In fibrosis (B), the area may be less affected, but the barrier is thickened by scar tissue, so the distance rises. Either lowers the rate at which carbon monoxide (or O2) moves from the alveoli into the blood. The test uses carbon monoxide because it binds haemoglobin so tightly that the blood’s partial pressure stays near zero: its uptake is limited by diffusion alone, not by blood flow.

This week you hand in four short answers instead of an essay. Each is marked out of five, against a list of points, so precision and coverage matter more than argument.

Answering a short-answer question

  1. Read the verb. “Distinguish” wants the differences, side by side; “explain” wants mechanisms; “interpret” wants a judgement from the data, justified.
  2. Count the marks. Five marks means at least five separate, precise points, in bullet points.
  3. Show your working for any number, with units, and say what the number means.
  4. Answer the question that’s asked. A labelled sketch (a dissociation curve, say) can save words.
Setting out an answer that uses data

State the result, with the working. “FEV1/FVC = 1.3 / 3.4 = 0.38.”

Compare it with a reference. “This is below 0.7, so the pattern is obstructive.”

Interpret it. “Air leaves the lungs slowly: the airways are narrowed, or collapse early in expiration.”

Explain the mechanism, and say what else would help. “In emphysema, loss of elastic recoil and of the alveolar walls that tether the small airways moves the equal pressure point into them. A high TLC and a low diffusing capacity would support this.”

Each line can earn a mark. Answers that jump straight to a diagnosis often earn one.
