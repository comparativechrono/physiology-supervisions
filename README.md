# Physiology supervisions

**Supervisions for NST IB Physiology with Tim Hearn, built on the [BMS workbench](https://github.com/comparativechrono/BMS-workbench)**, in the same way as the [BMS supervisions](https://github.com/comparativechrono/BMS-supervisions). The landing page has a tile for each supervision. Each supervision's page holds the work to hand in – an essay, or in two weeks four short answers, written in the workbench's *Supervision work* tab as students will write in the online exam, then handed in from the same page, as a Word document, straight to the supervisor's Google Drive – and short, optional exercises in the workbench, with the instructions alongside: a virtual microscope, the human body in 3D, real recordings of people, an organ bath, flow diagrams that run, circuits, PhET simulations, molecules in 3D and RStudio.

Everything runs in the student's web browser. Students don't need to install anything, there is no server-side code, and their files, answers and essays are stored in their own browser. The site is static (HTML, CSS, JavaScript and the data: slides, models, recordings and simulations), so it is hosted for free on **GitHub Pages**.

**All eight supervisions are ready** ([The supervisions](#the-supervisions)): for each, a piece of work to hand in, with no time limit; about half an hour of optional exercises in the workbench; and the rest of the week's essay titles, for revision, which students can hand in too if they would like feedback. Handing in is connected: work goes to the site's own hand-in script, which saves it in Tim Hearn's Google Drive ([Handing in](#handing-in)).

## The site

| Page | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| `index.html` | The landing page: a tile for each supervision, showing how far the student has got with its exercises and what they have done with its work to hand in (started, submitted, handed in), and the student's name, which goes on the work they hand in (every page of the site shares it) |
| `supervision-N.html` | A supervision's page (Supervisions 1 to 8): its chapters on the left and the workbench on the right, opening on Supervision work |
| `workbench.html` | The workbench's guide: a tour of its tabs (the BMS workbench's starter practical), linked from the landing page. A supervision whose work isn't ready yet opens it from its tile |

Each supervision's page is the workbench, with that supervision's chapters. Its first tab, **Supervision work** (the BMS workbench's *Exam practice*, renamed and moved to the front), lists the supervision's own papers first – the work to hand in, marked *To hand in*, then the optional papers – then the practice papers, and, closed, the other supervisions' papers. A paper to hand in has no time limit, and here suggests no time either: its clock counts the time spent writing, and locks nothing. **Handing in is built in**, as at the end of an exam: after submitting, the student types the hand-in password that the supervisor gives them (it is not on the site) and presses *Hand it in*, and the Word document – named after the paper and the student, with their name at the top – goes to the site's hand-in script, which saves it in Tim Hearn's Google Drive and sends back a receipt ([Handing in](#handing-in)). For the landing page, the site remembers when each supervision's paper to hand in was started, submitted, downloaded and handed in.

**How it differs from the BMS supervisions.** The workbench and the supervision pages work in the same way, from the same code. What is the site's own:

- its name, course and supervisor, and the tiles of the landing page (`build/supervisions.py`);
- where it saves things in the browser: everything has the prefix `physsup` (`storePrefix` in `config.js`), so the students' files, ticks, answers and name here are kept apart from the BMS workbench's (`bmswb`) and the BMS supervisions' (`bmssup`), although GitHub Pages serves all three from `comparativechrono.github.io`. Each page keeps its ticks and answers under its own name (its `data-page`), and the pages share the student's home folder and their name;
- its hand-in script, a Google Apps Script project of its own (`handin/`), with its own password, folder and sheet;
- its colours: navy, with a trace of the ECG as its mark, where the BMS sites are plum (the top bar in `assets/css/bms.css`, Supervision work and the calculator in `exam.css`, the landing page in `landing.css`, and the marks and icons in `build/template.html` and `build/landing.html`).

Each page has five chapters: *This week* (the work to hand in, the week's plan and the student's name); the two optional exercises; the work to hand in, with a point of writing for that week's title (using equations, setting out short answers, comparing, diagrams, answering from data, experimental evidence, a title in parts, weighing two systems) and the steps of writing, submitting and handing it in; and the revision titles. Only the name and the work to hand in count towards finishing the page. The chapters are in `build/s1.py` to `build/s8.py`, with what they share in `build/sup.py`, and nothing in them is copied from the handouts or the slides.

**Where the titles come from.** The endocrinology, renal and pH handouts suggest essay titles. Supervisions 3, 6 and 8 hand in one of them, and Supervision 7 the title in the fourth renal lecture's slides; each handout's other titles are the revision titles, with the renal handout's three quotations and the old Part IA question in the second renal lecture's slides. The pH handout's first title is corrected ("By what mechanisms is the pH of the blood regulated?", where the handout reads "What mechanisms is…"). The cardiovascular and respiration handouts suggest no titles, so Supervision 1's essay is Tim Hearn's title, and Supervision 4's essay, the short-answer questions of Supervisions 2 and 5 and the revision titles of Supervisions 1, 2, 4 and 5 were written for the site, from the lectures and their learning outcomes. Each paper's guidance, which students see once they have submitted, sets out the ground a good answer covers; it isn't a model answer.

**No time is given for the work to hand in.** Unlike the BMS supervisions, nothing says how long to spend on it: its papers have `"minutes": null` and no `suggestedMinutes`, so Supervision work shows *No time limit*, and the pages give no time for it. The optional exercises say roughly how long they take, and each tile says *Optional exercises: about half an hour*.

**The revision titles can be handed in.** Each supervision's revision paper (`exams/sN-revision.json`) is optional, but has `"handIn": true`: a student can write one title, leave the others blank, submit, and hand it in for feedback, into the same folder as the week's work.

**The numbers in the model answers** were measured in the workbench – the organ bath, the model of the circulation, the recording of standing up, the HPA and acid–base diagrams, the calculator and R – and `test/t_sups.py` checks each of them.

## Handing in

The site sends the work a student hands in to a **hand-in script**: [handin/Code.gs](handin/Code.gs), a Google Apps Script web app that runs in Tim Hearn's Google account. It checks the hand-in password, saves the Word document in his Google Drive (in *Physiology supervision work – handed in*, a folder for each supervision), adds a line to a Google Sheet that lists the work handed in, and sends back a receipt, which the page shows. Students don't need a Google account. If the work can't be handed in, the page says why, and what to do. The paper's first page offers to hand it in later (or again), and the list of papers says whether each piece of work has been handed in.

**It is a script of its own, not the BMS supervisions' one.** The BMS script would take work from this site too (all the GitHub Pages sites of the account share one address, and the script doesn't look at where a hand-in comes from), but the physiology work would then land in the BMS folders, under the BMS password, and in the BMS list. A second project keeps the courses apart, and leaves the BMS hand-in exactly as it is. It is set up ([handin/README.md](handin/README.md) says how: a new project at script.google.com, the password in its Script properties, `setup` run once, and a deployment as a web app), and its address is in `assets/js/config.js` as `handIn.script`. Opened in a browser, the address answers `{"ok":true,"service":"Physiology supervisions hand-in","version":1,"ready":true}`.

**Without an address in `config.js`** (`script: ''`), nothing is handed in through the site: a paper to hand in is written and submitted as usual, its receipt says that nothing has been sent and that the student should download the Word document and send it, and nothing asks for a password. (`test/t_site.py` checks both ways, and hands nothing in: a stand-in answers for the script.)

## Changing or adding a supervision

To change a supervision, edit its papers (`exams/sN-*.json`: the titles, instructions and guidance) or its chapters (`build/sN.py`), and run `python3 build/make_page.py`. The chapters read the titles from the papers, so a title is written in one place only. To add one:

1. **Its papers.** One JSON file for each paper, in `exams/`, named `sN-….json` and with `"week": N`. The paper to hand in has `"required": true`, `"minutes": null` (no time limit) and `"handIn": true`, which hands it in the way that `config.js` sets up (`handIn`); the papers here suggest no time (no `suggestedMinutes`). Optional papers have `"required": false`, and `"handIn": true` if they can be handed in too, as the revision titles can. `exams/s1-essay.json` and `exams/s1-revision.json` are good ones to start from.
2. **Its chapters.** A module `build/sN.py` with a function `ALL()` that returns the chapters, using the helpers of `build/lib.py` and `build/sup.py` (see [Writing chapters](#writing-chapters)); `build/s1.py` is a model. Give the chapters, steps and questions ids of their own (`sN-…`, checked when the page is built), and put the extras inside `optional()`, so that they don't count towards finishing the page.
3. **Its data**, if it has any: files in `starter/` are copied into each student's home folder (increase `STARTERS_VERSION` in `build/make_page.py` when you add files after students have started), and diagrams and circuits go in `examples/`.
4. **Its entry** in `build/supervisions.py`: add it to `READY`, like `EXAMPLE` there – its `page` (`supervision-N.html`), `data_page`, `module`, `title`, `topic`, `subtitle`, `description`, the texts of its tile (`card`, `handin`, `handin_short`, `time`), `handin_paper` (the id of its paper to hand in) and `handin_noun` (what the tile's status calls that work: *Essay*, or *Practical and short answers*). `COUNT` there is how many tiles the landing page has.
5. Run `python3 build/make_page.py`, which writes `supervision-N.html` and the landing page; try it with `python3 serve.py`; and test it as a student would use it (`test/t_sups.py` does this for the eight supervisions).

## What's in the workbench

Each supervision's page, and the guide, is the workbench. The **instructions** are on the left: chapters made up of steps that tick themselves off as the student completes them, and questions with model answers (some of which check a numerical answer). The **workbench** itself is on the right, with eleven tabs. Students can hide the instructions (with the button at the top left, or <kbd>Ctrl</kbd>+<kbd>\</kbd>) so that the workbench fills the screen. On a phone or a tablet, the workbench opens on top of the instructions (the **Workbench** button), and ✕ at the right of its tabs goes back to them. When there isn't room for all the tabs' names, they are set in smaller type, and when even that doesn't fit, the tabs show only their icons and the open tab keeps its name (on the smallest phones, the open tab is shown by its colour). The page's **Help** shows which icon is which tab.

| Tab | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| **Supervision work** | The work set for the supervisions, written as in the online exam system (Inspera), so that students get used to writing in a browser before they have to. It lists the page's own supervision's papers first (the one to hand in at the top), then the practice papers and the other supervisions'. A paper has a start page, a timer – or, with no time limit, a clock that counts the time spent writing up to a suggested time – one question at a time with an essay editor (paragraph styles, bold, italic, underline, sub- and superscript, lists, indents, tables, special characters, formulas, drawings, pictures from the student's files in the workbench – a photograph taken with the microscope, say – or from their computer – a photo of a diagram drawn on paper – undo and backups), a word count with a limit, flags, question tiles, a calculator, resources (which can open slides in the microscope), a submission page, a receipt, and a review with the marking guidance. As in Inspera's exam browser, only what was copied in the exam can be pasted into it: text copied from anywhere else is refused. Answers save themselves as students type (in `~/essays`), and a submission can be downloaded as a **Word document**, as a PDF (by printing), as a web page or as text. Papers are JSON files (`exams/`), and *Write an essay* starts a free essay for any title. A paper to hand in says how on its first page and after it is submitted, and puts the student's name on its Word document. |
| **Microscope** | A virtual light microscope. The **slide box** holds 31 slides, sorted by the system of the body: 29 sections of human tissue stained with H&E, from the GTEx project (normal tissues, except a coronary artery with atherosclerosis), and two slides drawn for practising measurement (a stage micrometer and a haemocytometer with living and dead cells). **Objectives** from 1× to 40× (keys 1 to 6) follow the convention of digital microscopy – 20× shows a slide at the full resolution of its scan, 0.5 µm per screen pixel – with a scale bar, a readout of the magnification and the field of view, and a map of the whole slide. The **eyepiece view** shows a round field of view with a graticule of 100 divisions, which students calibrate against the stage micrometer. The tools **measure** lengths and areas, **count** cells in up to six categories, and add **labels**; the camera takes **pictures** with a scale bar and the slide's credit, which keep their scale when they are opened again. Many slides carry teaching **labels** (hidden at first, so that students look for themselves), and the instructions can ask students to **find** a labelled structure and click on it (*Show me* takes them to an example and rings it). Every measurement and count is kept in `~/microscope/measurements.csv` and `~/microscope/counts.csv`, ready for R, and the microscope opens a ready-made R script for them. Students can also open a picture of their own (a photograph through a microscope, say) as a slide, and give it a scale from its scale bar. |
| **Recorder** | A chart recorder for real recordings of people, from the open-access databases of PhysioNet: 14 excerpts – the ECG (sinus rhythm, a 12-lead ECG, ventricular ectopic beats, the onset of atrial fibrillation, second-degree heart block), heart sounds with the ECG, blood pressure on standing up, the pulse (PPG) with breathing, heart rate and breathing (respiratory sinus arrhythmia), heart rate during exercise, needle EMG (healthy, myopathy, neuropathy) and the EEG with the eyes open and closed. Stacked channels on one time axis, zoom and scroll (and an overview of the whole recording), **Play** to replay a recording in real time as if it were being recorded (with sound for the heart sounds and the EMG), two-cursor **calipers** (Δt, the rate 60 ÷ Δt per minute and in Hz, the change and the lowest, highest and mean value of a channel), a marker, a **heart-rate channel** worked out beat by beat, the beat labels and rhythms given by cardiologists, a **data pad** of measurements – each an R–R, PR, QRS, QT interval or other, with the heart rate from the R–R intervals – kept as `~/recorder/measurements.csv`, CSV export of a recording (and its beats) for R, scripts for R, and pictures. |
| **Organ bath** | Isolated-tissue pharmacology, simulated: a length of guinea-pig ileum or a ring of rat aorta (with or without its endothelium) in an organ bath, drawn with its transducer, aeration and water jacket. Students add drugs by volume from stock solutions (the bath concentration is worked out exactly, C<sub>stock</sub> × *v* / (*V* + *v*), and cumulative doses add up), watch the tension on a chart-recorder trace with the doses marked under it, wash out, and let time run at ×1, ×5 or ×20 (or skip to the plateau, or wait 10 minutes for an antagonist to equilibrate). Each response is measured automatically, when the bath is washed or the next drug is added, into a **Results** table – kept as `~/organbath/<tissue>.csv` – and **Analyse in R** writes and opens a script that fits Hill curves with `nls()`, works out EC<sub>50</sub>s, dose ratios and pA<sub>2</sub>, and draws a Schild plot. Agonists, a partial agonist, competitive, non-competitive and irreversible antagonists, an anticholinesterase, nicotine and a ganglion blocker, nitric oxide and its synthase, KCl, and two unknown drugs to identify. The responses come from the operational model of agonism with noise, kinetics and differences between tissues: each fresh tissue has a random seed of its own, so every student's tissues (and unknown drugs) differ, and a button in the instructions can give a seed so that everyone has the same one ([build/organbath/README.md](build/organbath/README.md)). It is a teaching simulation, not data from real tissues. |
| **Diagrams** | Flow diagrams of physiological control: boxes for the quantities, stimuli, set points and comparators, and arrows that stimulate (+) or inhibit (−), each with a strength and a delay. **Run** turns the diagram into a simple model and plots how each quantity changes with time, so that students can see what negative and positive feedback, feed-forward and delays do (and, for a loop that oscillates, its period from one peak to the next). Boxes can be knocked out or held fixed. Diagrams are saved in `~/diagrams` (`.flow.json`) and can be exported as pictures (PNG or SVG) and their results as a table for R (CSV). Examples: feedback in temperature control, feed-forward, positive feedback in clotting, a delayed loop that oscillates, blood glucose, and a template for an endocrine axis. |
| **Circuits** | An electronics bench: batteries, current sources, AC sources, resistors, capacitors, inductors, switches, diodes, voltmeters and ammeters, joined by wires, with a simulator that works out the voltages and currents as time passes, and traces that plot them. In **Physiology** mode the same parts become pressure and flow sources, resistances to flow, compliances, valves, pressure gauges and flowmeters (in mmHg and mL/s). Examples include Ohm's law, a voltage divider, resistances in series and in parallel, charging a capacitor, the membrane as an RC circuit, the parallel-conductance model of the resting membrane potential and the Windkessel model of the circulation. Circuits are saved in `~/circuits` (`.circuit.json`), and can be exported as pictures and their traces as CSV. |
| **Simulations** | A library of 13 **PhET Interactive Simulations** (University of Colorado Boulder) chosen for biomedical science – Neuron, Membrane Transport, Diffusion, Gas Properties, Under Pressure, pH Scale, Acid-Base Solutions, Molarity, Beer's Law Lab, Color Vision, Geometric Optics, Capacitor Lab: Basics and Least-Squares Regression – kept with the site in the versions PhET published under CC BY 4.0, and run in the tab with PhET's usage statistics switched off. Search and topics, each simulation's screens, full screen, and an About box with what it is for, its authors, its version and its licence. |
| **Anatomy** | The human body in 3D, from **Z-Anatomy** (CC BY-SA 4.0; most of its models made from BodyParts3D): 12 systems (skeleton, muscles, heart and great vessels, arteries and veins, respiratory, digestive, urinary, male reproductive, endocrine glands, brain, spinal cord with nerves and eyes, lymphoid organs) and 2214 structures, each with its English name, nearly all (1993) with their Latin name and TA2 number, and four in five (1802) with the opening of a Wikipedia article. Turn the body round and zoom; show, hide or fade each system; click a structure to select it, then isolate it, fade the others round it or hide it; standard views (anterior, posterior, left, right, superior, inferior); sagittal, coronal and transverse sections with a slider (transverse ones seen from below, as CT and MRI show them); labels in the manner of an atlas; *find* tasks ("Find the thyroid gland and click on it"); a search by English, Latin or everyday names (*adrenal*, *windpipe*); pictures saved in Files (`~/anatomy`). Converted from Z-Anatomy's Blender file by `build/anatomy` (12 glTF files, 9 MB in all, each downloaded only when its system is first shown) and drawn with three.js. |
| **Molecules** | The structures of proteins and other molecules in 3D, drawn by NGL: cartoons, sticks, spheres and surfaces, colour schemes, selections, binding sites and their contacts, labels, distances and pictures. A **library** of 17 proteins of physiology (haemoglobin, myoglobin, insulin, a G-protein-coupled receptor with its G protein, steroid receptors, transporters, pumps and channels, and others) and 18 small molecules (hormones, transmitters, glucose, ATP, cAMP, haem…) is kept with the site; other entries are loaded from the Protein Data Bank or AlphaFold DB. |
| **RStudio** | An IDE modelled on RStudio, running R 4.6 in the browser (webR): scripts, R Markdown and Quarto, the Console, Environment, Plots, Packages, Help and Viewer panes, *Import Dataset*, and Shiny apps. The microscope's measurements and counts, the recorder's measurements and recordings, the organ bath's results, the results of diagrams and the traces of circuits are all CSV files that R reads. |
| **Files** | The student's home folder (`~`, which is `/home/user`): the folder tree (with upload, download – a folder downloads as a `.zip` file – and new, rename, move and delete), an editor, and viewers for tables, pictures, PDFs and web pages. Buttons above each file send it where it belongs: a diagram to Diagrams, a circuit to Circuits, a picture to the Microscope, an R script to RStudio, a structure to Molecules. |

## Before students use it

1. **Computers and browsers.** Students need a laptop or desktop computer with a current version of Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari; 8 GB of memory is comfortable. The workbench also works on a phone, although the screen is cramped (the simulations are best with the phone turned sideways). The Anatomy and Molecules tabs need WebGL (3D graphics), which almost every computer has.
2. **Network.** Students' browsers need to reach the site (the page and the slides) and, for the tabs that use them: `webr.r-wasm.org` (R itself, about 30 MB the first time RStudio is opened, then kept in the browser's cache) and `repo.r-wasm.org` (R packages, when they are installed); `models.rcsb.org` and `files.rcsb.org` (structures from the Protein Data Bank that are not in the library) and `alphafold.ebi.ac.uk` (AlphaFold models). The Microscope, Anatomy, Recorder, Organ bath, Diagrams, Circuits, Simulations, Supervision work and the library of the Molecules tab only need the site.
3. **Shared computers.** Students' files and essays are stored in the browser profile, so computers that wipe profiles when students log out will lose them. Tell students to download their work before they leave (*My files → Download all my files*, *My answers → Download my answers*, and *Download* on each submitted essay), and to use *Start afresh* when they finish on a shared computer.
4. **Supervision work is practice.** It works like the candidate's view of an online exam system, but it is not one: it is not locked down, it sends nothing to a server but the work that is handed in, and the student can see the model answers after submitting. Use it for practice and formative work, not for assessed exams. Whether the browser's spelling checker is on at the start is set for each paper (it is off by default, as it usually is in exams); students can switch it.
5. **Personal and patient data.** The slides are public, de-identified research images. If you add your own slides or pictures, make sure that you may publish them (anything on the site is public).
6. **Handing in.** Give the students the hand-in password (the one in the hand-in script's Script properties: [handin/README.md](handin/README.md)), and try a hand-in yourself before they do. For a new year, change the password there; the address stays the same.

## Publishing it on GitHub Pages

1. On GitHub, open the repository's *Settings → Pages → Build and deployment* page. Under Source choose **Deploy from a branch**, then choose the branch **main** and the folder **/ (root)**, and click Save. (The repository needs to be public, unless your GitHub plan supports Pages for private repositories.)
2. After a minute or two, the site will be available at `https://<owner>.github.io/<repository>/`, which for this repository is `https://comparativechrono.github.io/physiology-supervisions/`. That is the address to give to students: it opens the landing page.
3. The first time a browser opens the site, the page reloads itself once. This installs a small service worker (`isolation-sw.js`, next to `index.html`), which makes the page *cross-origin isolated*, so that R can wait for typed input and be interrupted with <kbd>Esc</kbd>; it also serves the Shiny apps that R runs.

**Size and bandwidth.** The site is about 415 MB: the slides take 360 MB (`slides/`, about 12 MB each), the simulations 33 MB (`sims/`, 1–6 MB each), the anatomy models 10 MB and the recordings 2 MB. Each is downloaded only when a student opens it. GitHub Pages allows a published site of up to 1 GB and has a soft limit of 100 GB of traffic a month. A student who looks at a slide only downloads the parts they look at, at the magnifications they use (the microscope asks for each tile of the slide with an HTTP *range request* into the slide's `tiles.pack` file, which GitHub Pages and most web servers answer), so a supervision with a few slides usually costs a few megabytes per student. If you add many more slides, keep an eye on the size of the repository (GitHub recommends staying under 1 GB) or host the slides elsewhere (see `slideBase` below).

The empty file `.nojekyll` stops GitHub from processing the site. The site itself doesn't need the `build/` and `test/` folders, but do keep `assets/vendor/licenses/`. To host the workbench on a different web server, just copy the folder (you can leave out `build/` and `test/`); the server must answer range requests (almost all do), and the address must use `https` (or be `localhost`) for the service worker to work.

**Students' files belong to the site's address.** The browser stores them for that address, so if the site moves to a different address, students will start with an empty home folder there. All the GitHub Pages sites of one account share the same address (`https://<owner>.github.io`), which is why this site has its own `storePrefix` (below).

## Trying it on your own computer

Opening `index.html` directly from your disk (`file://…`) doesn't work, because browsers block the Web Workers and the data files. Instead, serve the folder with a local web server:

```bash
cd physiology-supervisions
python3 serve.py 8000        # then open http://localhost:8000
```

`serve.py` answers range requests (which the microscope needs) and sends the cross-origin isolation headers itself. Python's own `python3 -m http.server` does **not** answer range requests, so the microscope only works with it after downloading each slide in full.

## Writing chapters

The supervisions' chapters are written like the BMS workbench's practicals. [Changing or adding a supervision](#changing-or-adding-a-supervision) says which files to change; this section describes the chapters themselves. This site's `build/s1.py` to `build/s8.py` are models for most of what a supervision's page does: a hand-in box, buttons that open diagrams, slides, parts of the body, molecules, recordings and papers, R typed into the Console for the student, multiple-choice questions with feedback, model answers, and extras that don't count.

The chapters, the papers' instructions and guidance and the landing page's notes are written in the supervisor's own voice, in the first person ("the hand-in password I give you", "my Google Drive"), in full sentences and British English. The site's own messages, in Supervision work and in the tiles, name the supervisor instead, from `handIn.to` in `config.js`.

### The chapters

The chapters are written in Python – the guide's in `build/chapters.py`, each supervision's in `build/sN.py` – using the helper functions in `build/lib.py`. Running `python3 build/make_page.py` puts them into the pages (between the `<!--CHAPTERS-->` and `<!--/CHAPTERS-->` markers in `build/template.html`), writes the landing page from `build/landing.html`, and also writes the lists that the tabs read (`slides/index.json`, `exams/index.json` and `examples/index.json`). It reports an error (and exits with an error status, after writing the pages) if two tasks, questions or headings of a page have the same name. Each module's `ALL()` lists its chapters in order. A chapter can also be kept in a file of its own, `build/ch_<name>.py`, with a function `chapter(num)` that returns it, as the chapters of the Anatomy, Recorder, Organ bath and Simulations tabs are (inside such a file, call the helper as `lib.chapter(...)`, since the file's own function is called `chapter` too).

## Slides, models, recordings, simulations, papers and examples

**Slides** live in `slides/<id>/` (an image pyramid of WebP tiles packed into one file, with `slide.json` describing it); `build/make_page.py` lists them in `slides/index.json`, sorted by system. [build/slides/README.md](build/slides/README.md) explains how the GTEx slides were chosen and made, how to label a slide for *find* tasks, and how to add a slide of your own (`build/slides/add_image.py`, from a photograph with a known scale or from a slide scanner's file).

**The models of the Anatomy tab** are in `anatomy/`: one glTF file for each part of the body (the skeleton, the muscles, the heart, the vessels…), `index.json`, which lists the 2214 structures (their ids, English and Latin names, TA2 numbers, systems and groups), and `definitions.json`. They were made from Z-Anatomy's Blender file by the scripts in `build/anatomy/` ([build/anatomy/README.md](build/anatomy/README.md) explains how, what was simplified and what was left out, and why); `build/anatomy/config.json` says which structures go into which file. A *find* task or a *show me* button names a structure by its id in `index.json`.

**The recordings of the Recorder** are in `recordings/`: for each, a `.json` file (its channels, units, sampling rate, annotations, where it comes from and how to cite it) and a `.bin` file (the samples, as 16-bit numbers), listed in `recordings/index.json`. They are excerpts of PhysioNet's open-access databases, made by `build/recordings/make_recordings.py` from the list in `build/recordings/recordings.json` (the script checks each download against the database's checksums); [build/recordings/README.md](build/recordings/README.md) gives each recording's source, licence, citation and what was changed. To add a recording, add an entry to `recordings.json` (only from a database whose licence allows it) and run the script. `recordingBase` in `config.js` lets another site hold them.

**The simulations** are in `sims/`, one folder for each (PhET's file, unchanged, and a picture), listed in `sims/index.json`, which `build/sims/fetch_sims.py` writes from `build/sims/sims.json`. Each is pinned to the last version PhET published under CC BY 4.0: **don't replace a simulation with PhET's latest version**, which is licensed CC BY-NC (see [build/sims/README.md](build/sims/README.md)). To add a simulation, add it to `sims.json` with the version to use, and run `fetch_sims.py`, which refuses a file that doesn't carry the CC BY 4.0 licence.

**The organ bath** has no data files: its tissues, drugs and their values are in `assets/js/organbath.js`, and [build/organbath/README.md](build/organbath/README.md) gives the model, the values and where they come from.

**Papers of Supervision work** are JSON files in `exams/` (the format is described at the top of `assets/js/exam.js`): a title, the time allowed (or `"minutes": null` and `suggestedMinutes`: no limit, and a suggested time), instructions, resources (the calculator, slides, links) and questions – information pages, essays (with a word limit, which can be a hard limit), short answers, numerical questions (with units and a tolerance) and multiple-choice questions – each with marking guidance that students see when they review their submitted answers. Pasting is kept within the exam unless a paper says `"clipboard": "any"` (as an exam taken in an ordinary browser allows). A supervision's papers have `week` (its number); the one to hand in also has `required: true` and `handIn`: `true` to hand it in through the hand-in script that `config.js` sets up, an object for a hand-in of its own (`{"script": …, "to": …, "password": true}`, which adds to `config.js`'s), or HTML that only says how to hand it in. The site comes with the BMS workbench's three practice papers (*Getting to know exam practice*, *Practice essay (45 minutes)* and *Calculations*). Write a new paper for each essay you set, and open it from the chapters with `exam('its-id')`.

**Examples of diagrams and circuits** are the `.flow.json` and `.circuit.json` files in `examples/diagrams/` and `examples/circuits/`, offered in each tab's Examples menu. The easiest way to make one is to build it in the tab, save it, download it from Files, and copy it into the folder (give it a `title`, a `description`, a `group` and an `order`). This site adds Guyton's model of the circulation in exercise (`exercise-circulation.circuit.json`, from the BMS supervisions), the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (`hpa-axis.flow.json`) and acid–base balance, with the lungs and the kidneys (`acid-base.flow.json`).

**The Molecules tab's library** is in `molecules/` (BinaryCIF files of the proteins, SDF files of the small molecules), and the list is in `assets/js/viewer-bms.js`. [molecules/README.md](molecules/README.md) says where they came from and how to add one.

## What students keep

Students' files, ticks, answers, essays and settings are stored in the browser's storage for the site (IndexedDB and local storage) on their own computer, and they are still there when the student comes back in the same browser. Nothing is stored on a server, and nobody else can see them. A different browser, a different computer or a private window won't have them, and clearing the browser's data for the site deletes them. *My files → Download all my files* downloads everything as a `.zip` file, *My answers → Download my answers* downloads the answers as a web page, and each essay can be downloaded as a Word document. The page warns students if it is open in two tabs of the same browser, because both would write to the same files.

## Privacy

The site sets no cookies and has no tracking or analytics. The only things that leave a student's computer are requests for the page and its slides, models, recordings and simulations (from the site); R and its packages (from `webr.r-wasm.org` and `repo.r-wasm.org`), when RStudio is used; structures that are not in the library (from RCSB PDB and AlphaFold DB), when the student asks for them; anything the student's own R code requests; and the work that a student hands in. The page sends it to the site's hand-in script in Tim Hearn's Google account – the Word document, the student's name (and candidate number, if they gave one), the paper, its number of words, when it was submitted, the address of the page it came from, and the hand-in password, which the page doesn't keep – and the script keeps the document, and a line about it, in his Google Drive. Shiny apps run inside the page. The PhET simulations are opened with PhET's usage statistics switched off (`?yotta=false`), so they contact neither PhET nor Google.

## Browser support and limitations

The workbench works in current versions of Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari. It was tested in Chromium; please try the browsers your students use, particularly Safari.

- **The slides** were scanned with a 20× objective (about 0.49 µm per pixel), so 40× enlarges the scan without showing finer detail, and fine structures that need oil immersion (bacteria, the fine structure of chromosomes) can't be seen. The slides are regions of a few millimetres cut from each GTEx section, not the whole section. They are sections of normal tissue (apart from the coronary artery, which has atherosclerosis), mostly from donors after death, so some show the changes of autolysis or of age (the slide's *About* panel gives the GTEx pathologist's notes).
- **The Anatomy tab**'s body is a man's (BodyParts3D was made from one man), so there are no female reproductive organs. The ear, the inside of the kidney and the cerebral white matter are left out because of their licences, and the teeth, joints, skin, meninges, pleura and peritoneum to keep the models small and clear. Four structures in five (1802 of 2214) have a definition. The models are of one man, so some organs lie where his did rather than where textbooks put them: his thyroid gland sits high, on the larynx. A section shows the inner surfaces of the organs it cuts (the cut faces are not filled), and the thinnest nerves and vessels are easier to find with the search box than by clicking.
- **The recordings** are short excerpts (13 seconds to 10 minutes) of PhysioNet's databases, with the annotations those databases give. In five of them (the 12-lead ECG, atrial fibrillation, the heart sounds, the blood pressure on standing and the pulse), the beats were found by a detector when the recordings were made and checked by eye, not by a cardiologist (their *About* panels say so). The ECG during exercise and both channels of the heart sounds are in arbitrary units (their databases give no calibration); in the atrial fibrillation recording (an ambulatory recorder's leads) the P waves are small and the fibrillation waves too small to see, so the R–R intervals carry the contrast; and the EEG's eyes-open and eyes-closed parts are two recordings put side by side (the Recorder marks the join). Sound plays at the chosen speed, so ×2 raises its pitch.
- **The organ bath** is a teaching simulation: its responses come from a model with typical, rounded values (with noise and differences between tissues), not from real tissues, and only nicotine desensitises. It has two preparations (the guinea-pig ileum and the rat aorta), and the unknown drugs are for the ileum only.
- **The simulations** are PhET's versions from before 29 March 2026 (the last under CC BY 4.0), so they won't receive PhET's later fixes; they are the English versions only. PhET designs them for a wide screen, so on a phone they are best turned sideways. A few steps of the guide tick themselves from values inside the simulations, which a new version could rename (the tests check them).
- **Handing in.** The hand-in script is the supervisor's own: it runs in his Google account, within Google's quotas for Apps Script, and hands in one essay at a time (if many arrive at once, a student may be asked to try again in a moment). Anyone with its address and the password could hand something in, so give the password only to the students, and change it each year. Every hand-in is kept (nothing is replaced) and listed in the sheet, with the student's name.
- **Supervision work** imitates the candidate's view of an online exam system; the real system may differ in its details, and it changes from year to year. It runs entirely in the browser: there is no lockdown, invigilation or plagiarism check (the page refuses pastes and drops of text copied elsewhere, but nothing stops a student retyping it), the clock is the computer's own, and the answers and marking guidance of a paper are in its file in `exams/` (so a determined student can read them). Formulas go into Word documents as text with sub- and superscripts, not as equation objects, and the spelling checker is the browser's own. Pictures are made smaller in the browser – at most 1600 pixels on their longer side, and 700 KB – and an answer can have up to 10 of them; a photo in the HEIC format of iPhones doesn't open in every browser, so it may need saving as a JPEG first (an iPhone itself usually sends a JPEG when a picture is chosen in a web page). The page says so when a picture can't be opened.
- **R in the browser** is R 4.6 (webR 0.6.0): one thread, a few gigabytes of memory, packages from the webR repository (most of CRAN, including ggplot2 and dplyr), and documents knitted to HTML only. RStudio's tab is an imitation made for teaching.
- **Cross-origin isolation.** R can only wait for typed input and be interrupted with <kbd>Esc</kbd> if the page is cross-origin isolated. GitHub Pages can't send the headers for this, so `assets/js/isolation.js` registers `isolation-sw.js`, a service worker that adds them (it doesn't cache anything). On a student's first visit, the page reloads itself once. Adding `?isolation=off` to the address removes the service worker.
