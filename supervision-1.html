Optional: what the endothelium does to arterioles

≈ 15 min

Repeat, in the Organ bath, the experiment that showed that the endothelium relaxes the muscle under it, and find out how.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with Dr Fraser’s second lecture, on the control of arteriolar smooth muscle.

In 1980 Robert Furchgott and John Zawadzki noticed that acetylcholine relaxed rings of rabbit aorta only if their endothelium hadn’t been rubbed off by accident. The endothelium, they concluded, releases something that relaxes the muscle under it. You can see the same thing in the Organ bath, and then find out what that something is. The ring here comes from a rat, and everyone gets the same one, so your numbers will match mine. An aorta has no tone of its own in the bath, so you contract it first with phenylephrine, an agonist at the α1-adrenoceptors that sympathetic noradrenaline acts on.

An intact ring
  1. Set up the ring of rat aorta . It runs 20 times faster than real time, because the aorta responds slowly.
  2. Contract it with phenylephrine at 10−6 M , and skip to the plateau .
  3. Now add acetylcholine at 10−6 M , skip to the plateau again, and wash the bath out . Washing measures the response: the table under the chart shows what fraction of the phenylephrine tone relaxed.
The same ring, unable to make nitric oxide
  1. Put L-NAME, which inhibits nitric oxide synthase, into the solution you wash the bath with, at 10−4 M . Give it 20 minutes to act .
  2. Contract the ring with phenylephrine again , skip to the plateau, then add acetylcholine at 10−6 M again and skip to the plateau.
  3. Without washing, add sodium nitroprusside, which releases nitric oxide itself, at 10−7 M . Skip to the plateau and wash. The table now has both responses in L-NAME.
What did acetylcholine do before and after L-NAME, and what did nitroprusside do? What do the three results show, taken together?
Before L-NAME, acetylcholine relaxed about four-fifths of the phenylephrine tone (about 83%). After 20 minutes in L-NAME it relaxed less than a fifth (17 to 19%), yet nitroprusside then relaxed over 90% of it. So acetylcholine relaxes the muscle by making the endothelium produce nitric oxide: block nitric oxide synthase and the relaxation largely goes. The muscle itself can still relax perfectly well, as nitroprusside shows, because it gives the muscle nitric oxide directly, which activates soluble guanylyl cyclase, raises cGMP and lowers the Ca2+ sensitivity and Ca2+ of the muscle. (Acetylcholine acts on M3 receptors of the endothelium, which raise its Ca2+ and so activate eNOS.) A ring whose endothelium has been rubbed off behaves like the ring in L-NAME, which is the original observation. That a little relaxation survives L-NAME is consistent with the endothelium having other dilators too, such as prostacyclin and endothelium-derived hyperpolarisation.
In the organ bath, KCl at 60 mM contracts the aorta. Yet in exercising muscle, a rise in interstitial K+ to about 10 mM within seconds dilates the arterioles, and is one of the main causes of the fast phase of functional hyperaemia. Why the difference?
Nitric oxide is one of many dilators released in exercising muscle. Yet blocking nitric oxide synthase alone changes the size of exercise hyperaemia surprisingly little. Why might that be, and what does it mean for how you write about the mechanisms in your essay?
The maintained phase of exercise hyperaemia has several causes acting at once: K+, adenosine (acting on A2A receptors, cAMP and KATP channels), ATP and nitric oxide released from red cells as they give up their O2, β2-adrenoceptor activation by circulating adrenaline, and nitric oxide from endothelium stimulated by the faster flow. They’re redundant: if one is blocked, the others make a bigger contribution, and some changes (a fall in PO2, say) alter the others. So an experiment that blocks one mediator and sees little change doesn’t show that the mediator does nothing. In the essay, distinguish the fast phase, whose causes are clear (K+ and the muscle pump), from the maintained phase, whose causes overlap, and say what kind of evidence there is for each, rather than listing dilators as if each were the cause.

Optional: exercise in a model of the circulation

≈ 15 min

Run Dr Fraser’s two equations as a model of the circulation, and find out what has to change for the cardiac output to rise five-fold without the right atrial pressure falling.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with Dr Fraser’s third lecture, on the determinants of cardiac output, and his fifth, on exercise.

Dr Fraser’s two equations make a model of the circulation that you can run. The mean systemic filling pressure (MSFP) is a pressure source, which drives venous return to the right atrium through the resistance to venous return (RvR), so that VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR. The heart is a flow source, which pumps into the arteries whatever flow you set. The blood flows back through the arterioles, the total peripheral resistance (TPR), so that ABP = CO × TPR. The tab shows pressures in mmHg, flows in mL/s (5 L/min is 83.3 mL/s) and resistances in mmHg·s/mL.

A model, not a body

The veins of this model are rigid, so venous return keeps rising however far the heart pulls RAP down. Real veins collapse once the pressure in them falls 1 or 2 mmHg below zero. A real heart also sets its own output: by Starling’s law it pumps what returns to it, which keeps RAP close to zero. Here you set its output yourself.

Rest, then exercise
  1. Open the model and run it . Venous return and cardiac output are both 83.3 mL/s (5 L/min), RAP is close to 0 mmHg, and ABP is 93 mmHg.
  2. Start exercising: click the switch Exercising muscles to close it. That puts the working muscles’ dilated arterioles alongside the others, and TPR falls to a fifth of its resting value. Watch ABP.
  3. Can the heart put this right on its own? Click the Heart, and in the panel on the right set its flow to 417 mL/s, five times its resting output. Watch RAP.
  4. Now the veins. Click Mean systemic filling pressure and raise it from 7 to 17.5 mmHg, as sympathetic venoconstriction would. Then close the switch Muscle pump, which halves RvR.
With the muscles dilated, ABP fell to about 19 mmHg. With the heart pumping 417 mL/s on its own, ABP was back to 93 mmHg, but what happened to RAP, and why couldn’t a real heart do this? What finally brought RAP back to zero?
ABP = CO × TPR, so with the output unchanged ABP fell to a fifth, about 19 mmHg. Pumping five times as much restores ABP, but RAP falls to about −28 mmHg, because with MSFP and RvR unchanged, VR = (MSFP − RAP) / RvR can only rise five-fold if RAP falls that far: (7 + 28) / 0.084 = 417 mL/s. Real veins collapse long before that, so a heart that pumps harder can’t draw more blood out of them, which is why pacing the heart faster barely raises the cardiac output. Raising MSFP 2.5-fold (venoconstriction) and halving RvR (the muscle pump) lets venous return rise 2.5 × 2 = 5-fold with RAP back at zero. In the body, Starling’s law holds RAP near zero and the output follows venous return; the heart’s faster rate and greater contractility let it pump the larger return without RAP rising.
Dr Fraser’s handout says that in intense exercise the cardiac output can rise about five-fold, with the heart rate rising about three-fold. By roughly how many times must the stroke volume rise?
CO = HR × SV, so SV must rise by 5 / 3 ≈ 1.7 times. The handout’s figures of a three-fold rise in rate and a 50% rise in stroke volume give 3 × 1.5 = 4.5, close to five-fold. Stroke volume can rise because venous return and filling rise with it, and because sympathetic stimulation makes each beat more forceful, so the ventricle empties further.
In exercise, mean arterial pressure rises slightly, although TPR falls to a fifth. What drives the increase in sympathetic activity and the fall in cardiac vagal activity that achieve this?

“Discuss the cardiovascular adjustments that occur in whole body exercise.”

What “discuss” asks of you

“Discuss” asks for an argument, not a list. A good essay on this title doesn’t just describe each change in exercise. It explains why each one is needed, how it comes about, and how the changes fit together into one response. A useful way in is to see the whole response as solving one problem: the working muscles dilate their arterioles to get the blood they need, and the rest of the circulation has to cope with the consequences.

  • In the muscles. How does their blood flow rise, and how fast? What do the fast and the maintained phases of functional hyperaemia depend on, and how good is the evidence for each? What happens at the capillaries?
  • In the whole circulation. What does that do to TPR, and so to ABP? What must the cardiac output do, and how do venous return, the heart rate and the stroke volume rise to match it? Chapter 2 has numbers.
  • The control. What drives the changes in sympathetic and vagal activity, and why couldn’t the baroreflex do it alone? What happens to the blood flow to other organs: the gut, the kidneys, the skin, the heart and the brain?
  • The argument. What, in the end, limits how hard a fit person can exercise? Is it the same for someone with heart failure? An essay that ends on a well-argued point like this is better than one that just stops.

Three common slips

Each of these sentences is the kind that essays on this title often contain. Is each one right? If not, what’s wrong with it?

“During exercise, the sympathetic nervous system dilates the arterioles of the working muscles, which increases their blood flow.”
“Because total peripheral resistance falls, arterial blood pressure falls during exercise.”
“In exercise, more oxygen reaches the muscles only because their blood flow rises.”
