Optional: airways, vessels and alveoli

≈ 15 min

Find the parts of a lung that set its resistance, its compliance and its blood supply, and work out why surfactant matters.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with the first and second lectures, on compliance, airway resistance and the pulmonary circulation.

This section of human lung has a bronchiole, the branch of the pulmonary artery that runs beside it, and alveoli all round. Some of the alveoli have collapsed, as is common in lung taken after death.

Airway, artery and alveoli
  1. Open the lung at 2× .
  2. Find the bronchiole . It’s an airway with no cartilage in its wall, so nothing but the lung round it holds it open.
  3. Find the smooth muscle in its wall .
  4. Find the branch of the pulmonary artery beside it . Compare its wall with the lumen it surrounds.
  5. Find some alveoli , then measure the width of one open alveolus with the measuring tool .
The bronchiole has smooth muscle but no cartilage. What holds it open during a normal breath, and why can airways like it close during a forced expiration, or in emphysema?
It’s held open by the radial traction of the lung tissue attached round it (the alveolar walls pull outwards on it, more strongly as the lung expands), and by the pressure inside it being higher than the pressure round it. In a forced expiration, pleural pressure rises above atmospheric, and the pressure inside the airway falls along its length towards the mouth because of airway resistance. At the equal pressure point the pressure inside equals the pressure outside; downstream of it, the airway is squeezed (dynamic compression), so pushing harder doesn’t raise the flow: expiratory flow becomes effort-independent. Cartilage stops this happening in the large airways, but bronchioles have none. In emphysema the alveolar walls that tether the bronchioles are destroyed, so they lose their radial traction and collapse earlier, trapping air; smooth muscle contraction (asthma) narrows them further.
A water-lined alveolus of radius 100 µm would need a pressure of P = 2T / r to stay open, where T, the surface tension of water, is 0.07 N/m. Work it out in pascals. (1 cmH2O is about 98 Pa.) What does surfactant do to this, and why does it matter for small alveoli?
P = 2 × 0.07 / (100 × 10−6) = 1400 Pa, about 14 cmH2O, from surface tension alone. Surfactant (dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine, from type II pneumocytes) lowers T, and lowers it more the more the surface is compressed, so as an alveolus shrinks in expiration its surface tension falls and the pressure needed to keep it open doesn’t rise as 1/r would predict. Without surfactant, small alveoli would empty into larger ones and collapse (atelectasis), the lungs would be much stiffer (less compliant), and fluid would be drawn into the alveoli. That’s respiratory distress in premature infants, who haven’t yet made enough surfactant.
The branch of the pulmonary artery has a thin wall for its size, compared with a systemic artery of the same diameter. Why?

Optional: why mismatch lowers the arterial PO₂

≈ 15 min

Mix blood from a well-ventilated and a poorly ventilated part of the lung, in R, and find out why the good part can’t make up for the bad.

Optional, about 15 minutes

This exercise isn’t part of the work to hand in, and its steps don’t count towards finishing the page. It goes with the third lecture, on ventilation and perfusion matching.

Imagine a lung with only two units, each receiving half of the blood. One is well ventilated for its blood flow (a high V/Q), so its blood leaves at a PO2 of 120 mmHg. The other is poorly ventilated (a low V/Q), and its blood leaves at 45 mmHg. What is the PO2 of the arterial blood, once the two streams have mixed? It isn’t the average of the PO2s, because blood mixes by its O2 content, not its PO2. You need the dissociation curve, and R.

Mixing blood from two units, in R
  1. Write the oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve as a function. This is the Hill equation, with a P50 of 26.8 mmHg: sat <- function(po2) po2^2.7 / (po2^2.7 + 26.8^2.7)
  2. Now the O2 content, in mL per litre of blood, with 150 g/L of haemoglobin that carries 1.39 mL of O2 per gram, plus what is dissolved (0.03 mL/L per mmHg): content <- function(po2, hb = 150) 1.39 * hb * sat(po2) + 0.03 * po2
  3. Plot it curve(content(x), 0, 150, xlab = "PO2 (mmHg)", ylab = "O2 content (mL per litre)") . Notice where the curve is flat.
  4. Work out the content of the blood leaving each unit units <- content(c(120, 45)); units , and of the two mixed in equal amounts mixed <- mean(units); mixed .
  5. Find the PO2 that gives the mixed content uniroot(function(p) content(p) - mixed, c(1, 150))$root . (uniroot finds the PO2 at which the content minus the mixed content is zero.)
What is the PO2 of the mixed blood, to the nearest mmHg? Why is it so much lower than 82.5 mmHg, the average of the two units’ PO2s?
About 60 mmHg. The blood from the well-ventilated unit, at 120 mmHg, is on the flat top of the curve: its haemoglobin is already about 98% saturated, so its content (about 208 mL/L) is hardly more than at a normal 100 mmHg. Raising its PO2 further adds almost nothing. The blood from the poorly ventilated unit, at 45 mmHg, is on the steep part, at about 80% saturation and 169 mL/L. Mixed equally, the content is about 189 mL/L, which on the curve is a PO2 of about 60 mmHg. So a high V/Q unit can’t make up for a low V/Q one: any mismatch lowers the arterial PO2, and the arterial PO2 is lower than the average alveolar PO2, even in a healthy lung (by a few mmHg: a PO2 of about 100 mmHg in the alveolar capillaries, and about 95 in the systemic arteries).
In the same lung, the CO2 of the arterial blood can be kept normal, if the patient breathes harder. Why can high V/Q units make up for low ones for CO2, but not for O2?

Using diagrams in an essay

This title has three parts: why the mismatch exists in a healthy lung, how the lung limits it, and when the lung’s response does harm. Several of its ideas are clearer as a diagram than in words, and a good diagram, used well, earns marks. Used badly, it wastes time. A diagram earns its place when:

  • it shows something words can’t do as well: a gradient from the top of the lung to the bottom, the shape of a curve, or how pressures compare in West’s zones;
  • it’s labelled: axes with their quantities and units, the regions named, the key numbers marked;
  • it has a caption, a sentence that says what it shows;
  • the text refers to it and uses it: “As Figure 1 shows, in zone 1 alveolar pressure exceeds arterial pressure, so…”. A diagram the text never mentions might as well not be there.

Two or three diagrams are plenty. Some that would help with this title: the distribution of ventilation, blood flow and V/Q from the top of the lung to the bottom; West’s three zones, with PA, Pa and Pv; and the O2 dissociation curve, with the two units of chapter 2 marked on it.

Which of these would be the best use of a diagram in this essay?
  • Why the mismatch exists. Both ventilation and blood flow increase from the top to the bottom of the upright lung, but for different reasons (the gradient of pleural pressure; hydrostatic pressure, and West’s zones), and blood flow increases more steeply. So V/Q falls from the top (about 3) to the bottom (about 0.6).
  • Why it matters. Chapter 2: low V/Q units lower the arterial PO2, and high V/Q units can’t make up for them. Venous admixture (the bronchial circulation, and low V/Q units at the bases) is why arterial PO2 is lower than alveolar.
  • How the lung limits it. Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction diverts blood from poorly ventilated regions; low PCO2 constricts the airways of poorly perfused regions. Gravity, posture (prone positioning in ARDS) and disease all change the picture.
  • When the response does harm. In generalised hypoxia (altitude, lung disease) the same vasoconstriction becomes pulmonary hypertension: right ventricular strain and failure (brisket disease in cattle), and high-altitude pulmonary oedema.
